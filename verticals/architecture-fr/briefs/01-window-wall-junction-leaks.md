# Brief 01: no one owns the junction between window and wall

Architecture, France. Written 2026-11-16 from candidate C3. Marks: **[P]** primary source
read and figure seen in it, **[S]** search summary or secondary source.

## The problem

Leaks are the leading ten-year liability claim against French architects, and windows
are a growing share of them. A large part of those leaks starts where the window meets
the wall or the sill, in small works that belong to no trade's contract, so nobody
designs, prices, or checks them.

## Who has it

The architect has it as designer and site supervisor: 29,521 registered in 2023 [S],
and 91% of agencies supervise sites [P]. Insurers carry the cost first, led by the
architects' mutual (MAF) and the contractors' insurers. The installer and the client
share the consequences.

## The workflow and where it breaks

The architect draws the details and writes the specification by trade (*lot*), reviews
the contractors' execution drawings (VISA), and checks the work on site (DET). The
junction around a window needs pieces from several trades: the concrete upstand under
the frame (*rejingot*), sill flashing, level corrections, the seal. The break is in who
owns them.

- The quality agency's study of window claims found that these complementary works "are
  not necessarily assigned to a particular trade" and called for coordination between
  contractors, architect, and client before work starts. [P] (AQC, *Fenêtres: points de
  vigilance*, 2019)
- Of 236 expert reports with an identified cause, 24% traced to the window-to-structure
  junction and 8% to the window's surroundings (a defective concrete upstand,
  ventilation faults wrongly blamed on the window, damage by other trades; the study
  gives no split). A further 12% traced to a sill too low to hold water back, chiefly
  the 20 mm thresholds required for wheelchair access; the study classes these as
  defects of the window itself. Manufacturing and hardware defects, which design cannot
  prevent, made up most of the rest. The 236 come from one expert firm in 2017 and
  represent under 5% of French window claims that year. [P] (same)
- External insulation makes it worse: water that once drained and dried behind interior
  lining now stays trapped, and "claims from leaks through the upstand are multiplying."
  [P] (same)
- In apartment buildings, window claims "nearly doubled in ten years, mainly through
  defects in sealing against their support." Flat-roof waterproofing claims more than
  doubled, which "shows great weakness in the treatment of singular points." Both
  statements describe shares of defect counts among building-damage claims between
  €1,915 and €162,000, not claim volume or cost. [P] (AQC observatory report 2026)
- The menuisier "often has the support imposed on him" and adapts for reasons of cost
  and time. [P] (AQC 2019, conclusion)

## How they cope today

Architects rely on the installation standard (NF DTU 36.5), the quality agency's free
technical and interface sheets, the insurer's toolkits [P], and, on larger buildings,
the technical inspector. For external insulation, a free guidance document (FD DTU
45.3, article 8) already tabulates which trade does which junction task, though it
cannot serve as a contract document [S].

The rule-makers are working on the same gap. The concept of "localised complementary
interface works" (OCIL) has sat in the forewords of five building standards since 2017:
such works fall outside every trade's contract unless the tender documents describe
them. The dedicated standard for the junction between openings and windows (XP
P18-202-3) and the revision of the window installation standard, both begun in 2017,
remain unpublished, with forecast dates in 2026 and 2027 that keep slipping. [S] (red
team, from the standards office's programme of November 30, 2025)

Neither I nor the red team (about 20 French queries) found a product or paid service in
France that checks junction details and their allocation to trades.

## Why now

External insulation and low accessibility thresholds multiply the junctions that leak,
and RE2020 demands tighter windows [P]. Gross payouts on building-damage cover rose 20%
in 2024 [P]. The pending standards will, once published, define what a compliant
junction is and who must describe it, which gives a checking tool a rule to check
against. Models can now read a detail drawing and a specification together.

## Neighbours

- **Belgium:** ten-year liability insurance has been mandatory for housing since July
  1, 2018, but it covers water tightness only where a leak endangers the building's
  solidity or stability [S]. The incentive is weaker than in France. No claims figures
  found.
- **Switzerland:** no mandatory insurance regime; liability runs under contract and the
  SIA 118 standard [S]. No figures found.
- **Luxembourg, Monaco:** unchecked.

## Solution directions

- **Junction check on the tender documents.** Reads detail drawings and the
  specification, lists each window, threshold, and roof junction, and flags every
  complementary work that no trade owns.
- **Trade-allocation matrix.** Generates the list of interface works for a project and
  writes them into the right trade's specification, so each is priced and owned.
- **Review as a service.** A former claims expert, with model support, reviews a
  project's junction details for a fixed fee. It needs no product.
- **Hold-point photo check.** Before a junction is covered, the installer photographs
  it and the architect's supervision record shows pass or fail against the detail.
- **Prevention programme for an insurer.** The same check paid for by the insurer and
  tied to the premium or to the penalty for claim-heavy members.

## The weakest points

1. **The party who can prevent the leak does not pay for it.** Claims surface years
   later and insurers pay. An architect's premium follows declared works, and the
   insurer adjusts it only for members whose claims outrun their premiums [P]. Without
   a premium credit, an agency whose fees are falling (49% in 2025 [P]) has little
   reason to buy.
2. **Each claim is small and the architect carries a minority of it.** A joinery leak
   claim averages about €5,300 [S], and in the one ruling found the installer's side
   bore 70%. The saving per project falls well short of a review fee unless windows
   fail in series, and serial claims are missing from the data.
3. **The architects' insurer's claims are falling.** Files opened fell 9.7% and payouts
   8.6% in 2025 [P]. The contractors' insurer, SMABTP, holds more of the cost and the
   data, and is the better first conversation.
4. **Most window claims are not design failures.** Manufacturing, hardware, and sagging
   sashes account for more than half of the 2017 sample. The addressable part is at
   most the 32% tied to surroundings and junction.
5. **The knowledge is free and the failure persists.** The study blames cost and time
   pressure on site, which a checking tool does not remove.
6. **A pending standard targets the same gap.** It may shrink the problem, or it may
   create the obligation a tool helps meet.
7. **Insurers are few, slow, and build prevention in-house.**

## Kill criteria

Drop the brief if any of these holds:

- An insurer's prevention unit puts junction and detailing failures below 15% of
  infiltration claim cost.
- No insurer among three approached would fund a check or credit the premium, and
  fewer than three of ten architects would pay €300 a project themselves.
- The red team finds a product or service already doing this in France.

## My own grade

Plausible. I first graded it worth testing; the red team showed that every large money
figure is a sector total and that the one figure tied to this problem is a small
average claim. The mechanism is real, specific, and confirmed at the source, and no one
sells a fix. The economics are the weak flank. One question to SMABTP's or MAF's
prevention unit would settle it: what share of leak claim cost starts at a junction no
trade owned, including serial claims, and would you pay to close it?
